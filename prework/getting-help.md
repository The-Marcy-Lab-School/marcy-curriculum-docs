# Where to Turn For Help

Getting help is part of the assignment. Strong engineers do not work alone in silence until everything is perfect. They use the resources around them, explain what they have tried, and ask questions that help everyone learn.

A good help process usually moves in this order:

1. **Computer:** Try to learn from tools, examples, documentation, search, or AI.
2. **Peer:** Talk through the problem with another fellow.
3. **Teacher:** Ask staff for help when you are still stuck, confused, or unsure what to try next.

You do not need to spend hours at each step. The goal is to make an honest attempt, collect useful information, and ask a clearer question.

## How do I know I need help?

Feeling confused does not mean you are bad at this. It usually means you are learning something hard.

A good engineer does not ask for help only after everything has gone wrong. A good engineer notices when they are stuck, names what is happening, and chooses a next step.

You might be productively struggling if:

- you have a specific thing you are trying to make happen,
- you are testing one small change at a time,
- each attempt teaches you something,
- and you can explain what you tried.

You might be stuck if:

- you are changing random blocks without knowing why,
- you keep watching tutorials but are not building,
- you cannot explain what you expected to happen,
- you have been in the same place for 20–30 focused minutes,
- or you feel yourself shutting down, avoiding the project, or thinking, “I’m just bad at this.”

When that happens, pause and say:

**This is hard because I am learning something new. I  can learn new things. I just need to pause, name what’s confusing, and choose my next step.**

Use the help ladder to figure out what that next step might be.

## Step 1: Ask the computer

Use this step when you need an example, a definition, a tutorial, or a reminder of how a Scratch block works.

### Google or search

Search can help, but not every result is equally useful. When choosing a source, ask:

- Is this from a trusted organization, like Scratch, MIT, Raspberry Pi Foundation, Khan Academy, or another education-focused source?
- Is the explanation about Scratch 3.0 or the current Scratch editor?
- Does the example actually match what I am trying to do?
- Can I understand the explanation well enough to explain it back?
- Is the page trying to teach me, or mostly trying to sell me something?

Search examples (always put “Scratch” in the search question):

- `Scratch how to use variables score`
- `Scratch broadcast when I receive example`
- `Scratch forever loop if touching sprite`
- `Scratch nested if else example`
- `Scratch game timer variable tutorial`

When you find an example, do not copy it blindly. Ask: **What problem is this solving, and what part of it applies to my project?**

### Claude or another AI tool

AI can be useful for explaining ideas, generating examples, or helping you debug. But do not read the answer passively. Your job is to build understanding.

A good AI prompt might be:

> I am building a Scratch collector game. I want the score to go up when my player touches a star, but it keeps increasing too fast. Explain what might be happening in beginner-friendly language. Ask me one question before giving advice.
> 

After AI responds, use this “repeat back” move:

> My understanding is: the score keeps increasing because the player is touching the star for many moments inside a loop, not just once. Is that right?
> 

Then decide what to try next. If you cannot explain the suggestion in your own words, do not use it yet.

## Step 2: Ask a peer

Use this step when you have tried something and need another person to help you see what is happening.

A peer can help by:

- watching you run the project,
- asking what you expected to happen,
- reading your blocks with you,
- helping you compare two versions,
- or explaining how they solved a similar problem.

Good peer questions sound like:

- “Can I explain what I think is happening, and you tell me where I’m losing you?”
- “Can you watch this run and tell me what you notice?”
- “I expected the score to change once, but it keeps changing. Do you see why?”
- “Can you show me where you used a broadcast in your project?”
- “Can I look inside your project to understand the structure? I won’t copy it.”

When helping a peer, do not just take over the keyboard. Ask questions, point to patterns, and help them keep ownership of their project.

## Step 3: Ask a teacher

Ask a teacher when:

- you have been stuck for more than 20–30 focused minutes,
- you tried a tutorial or example but still do not understand it,
- your project is not meeting a requirement and you are unsure what to change,
- you think your idea may be too big and need help simplifying,
- or you are behind and need help making a realistic plan.

A strong teacher question includes:

| Part of the question   | Example                                                                                        |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What I am trying to do | “I am trying to make my sprite lose one life when it touches an obstacle.”                     |
| What I expected        | “I expected the lives variable to go down by 1.”                                               |
| What actually happened | “It goes down very quickly until it reaches 0.”                                                |
| What I tried           | “I tried moving the `change lives by -1` block outside the loop, but then it stopped working.” |
| What I need help with  | “Can you help me understand where the condition should go?”                                    |

## “Asking a question helps the room”

If you are confused, someone else probably is too. Asking a clear question does not slow the room down — it often helps the room move forward.

Use this norm when:

- a requirement is unclear,
- a Scratch concept still feels fuzzy,
- a tutorial skipped a step,
- you found a helpful resource others might use,
- or you solved a bug that others may run into.

Helpful room questions include:

- “Can someone explain what a broadcast is doing in plain language?”
- “Can we look at one example of a nested conditional?”
- “What is the difference between a loop and an event?”
- “I found a Scratch tutorial that helped with variables. Would it be helpful to share it?”

Asking for help is not separate from technical work. It is one of the technical habits this assignment is designed to help you practice.

## Avoiding tutorial hell: learn just enough to try

It is easy to get stuck watching tutorials, reading examples, searching, or asking AI for more explanations before you build anything. That can feel productive, but it often delays the part where real learning happens: trying something small and seeing what happens.

Use this rule:

**Learn just enough to try one small thing. Then build.**

Before opening another tutorial or asking for another explanation, ask:

1. What am I trying to make happen?
2. What is the smallest test I can try?
3. What do I already know that might help?
4. What is the one thing I need to look up?
5. After five or ten minutes, what did I try?

If you are using Google, Scratch examples, or AI, do not try to understand everything before you begin. Look for the next useful move, then return to your project.

A less helpful question is:

> “How do I make my whole game?”
> 

A stronger question is:

> “I am trying to make the score go up when the sprite touches the star. I tried this block sequence. The score is not changing. What should I check next?”
